Traffic but No Sales? The 12 Trust Signals Shoppers Check Before They Buy

Shopify traffic but no sales is the most asked question in every store feedback thread, and the answers are mostly app pitches. Do the arithmetic first, check the traffic second, then work the twelve trust signals shoppers look for, each with a two-minute test and a fix.

Your Shopify dashboard says 400 sessions this month. Your orders page says two. So you search for why your Shopify store has traffic but no sales, and the first ten results tell you to redesign your product pages, install a social proof app, and rebuild your checkout.

Before you do any of that, do the arithmetic. The average Shopify store converts about 1.4% of its sessions. At 400 sessions, that predicts roughly six orders. Two is low. Zero is not yet a signal. Neither number proves your store is broken.

That distinction matters, because the fix for "not enough data yet" is patience, and the fix for "shoppers do not trust this store" is twelve specific things you can check this afternoon. Most posts written for this search sell you the second answer without checking the first.

This one does both. First the math and the traffic check, so you know whether you have a conversion problem at all. Then twelve trust signals in the order a shopper meets them, each with a two-minute test and a fix.

For what it is worth, Studio Niza's own homepage is both its most visited page and its top exit page. I am not writing this from outside the problem.

Shopify traffic but no sales? Start with the math

Littledata benchmarked 2,800 Shopify stores and found an average conversion rate of 1.4%. Clearing 3.2% puts a store in the best 20%, and 4.7% puts it in the best 10%. Mobile visitors average 1.2% against 1.9% on desktop.

Take your sessions for the last 30 days and multiply by 0.014. Two hundred sessions predicts about three orders, five hundred predicts seven, a thousand predicts fourteen. Below roughly a thousand sessions, a zero is not evidence of anything except a small sample.

Once you have the expected number, find out where people stop. Shopify's Conversion rate breakdown report sits under Analytics, then Reports, then the Behavior category. It shows four stages: all sessions, sessions with cart additions, sessions that reached checkout, and sessions that completed checkout.

Three patterns, three different problems. If cart additions are close to zero, people are looking at your store and deciding not to start, which is a trust and information problem and the subject of the rest of this post.

If cart additions look healthy but few sessions reach checkout, something changed between the product page and the cart, usually shipping cost appearing for the first time. If people reach checkout and do not finish, the friction is inside checkout itself. Most stores asking this question have the first pattern.

Rule out the traffic before you touch the store

Three checks, about ten minutes. Do them before you change a single line of copy, because if the traffic is wrong, none of the twelve signals below will move your numbers.

Are these people human?

Shopify now labels every session as human or bot, and you can add a Human or bot session dimension to any sessions report and filter it to Human. The classification is deliberately conservative, and it only applies to data from October 7, 2025 onward, so older sessions cannot be reclassified.

If your session count drops noticeably when you filter, your real conversion rate was always better than your dashboard implied. New stores in particular pick up automated traffic that clusters in a handful of data centre cities, so a Sessions by location report topped by places you have never marketed to, with sessions lasting seconds each, is that traffic.

Are they the right people?

Look at sessions and conversion rate by referrer. Someone who typed a product question into Google arrived intending to buy something. Someone who stopped scrolling on a video did not, and asking them to buy in the same visit is a different job.

If one channel supplies most of your traffic and converts at zero while the others convert normally, you have a targeting problem rather than a store problem. That is an acquisition question, and no amount of trust work fixes it.

Where do they land?

Check Sessions by landing page. Ads pointed at the homepage make people hunt for the thing they were promised, and a fair share give up during the hunt. Send traffic to the page that matches the message that got the click.

If all three checks come back clean, and you have more than a thousand human sessions with almost no cart additions, you have a trust problem. Now the twelve signals are worth your Saturday.

Signals 1 to 3: the first five seconds

Open your store on your own phone, not your laptop. The best test I have seen a store owner get in a forum was this: look at it for five seconds and answer two questions. What am I buying here, and why should I trust this shop?

Signal 1: Can I tell what this store sells?

Two-minute test: hand your phone to someone who has never seen the store, let them look for five seconds, then take it back and ask what the shop sells and who it is for.

The fix: one plain sentence above the fold naming the product category and the person it is for. "Browse our latest products" is not that sentence. There is more in the post on what belongs above the fold on a Shopify homepage.

The related failure is range. A store selling dresses, phone accessories, and kitchen organisers reads as a general store, and nobody feels it was built for them. Narrowing the catalogue is free and answers the question faster than any headline.

Signal 2: Is there a real business behind this?

Two-minute test: scroll to your footer. Is there a business name, a location, and an email address on your own domain? Now open your About page. If you do not have one, that is the finding.

The fix: an About page with a real name, a real photo, and a plain explanation of why the shop exists, plus a contact address at your domain instead of a free mailbox. This is the most repeated advice experienced sellers give in store feedback threads, and it costs an hour.

Signal 3: Do the photos look like someone owns these products?

Two-minute test: reverse image search your own hero product photo. If the same image sits on twenty other stores, your shoppers can find that too.

The fix: at least one photo you took yourself, and at least one that shows scale. Baymard's testing found 42% of users try to work out how big a product is from the images alone, so a plain background with nothing for reference leaves that question open.

Signals 4 to 8: the product page

Baymard's latest benchmark rated 52% of desktop and 62% of mobile ecommerce sites as mediocre or worse on product page experience, and those are large, well-resourced retailers. What sinks a sale is rarely one fatal flaw. It is four small unanswered questions stacking up until buying stops feeling like a good idea.

Signal 4: Are there reviews, and are they recent?

Two-minute test: open a product page in a private browsing window so you see what a stranger sees. Count the reviews. Check the date on the newest one.

The fix: install a reviews app with a real free tier, Judge.me being the usual recommendation for stores under $200K a year, and ask your existing customers. Two or three genuine reviews beat zero by a wide margin, and there is more on the thresholds in the post on what shoppers need to see before they trust a rating. Reply to any negative ones. Baymard found 89% of sites never do, and shoppers read the bad reviews first. If collection is the part that keeps slipping, that is what the Reviews Management service runs.

Signal 5: What questions does the page leave open?

Two-minute test: write down every question you would ask a shop assistant holding this product. Materials, measurements, care, how it compares to the cheaper one. Then read your description and count how many it answers.

The fix: put the answers in the description rather than in a size chart three clicks away. The post on the questions a product description has to answer covers the structure, and if shoppers are choosing between two of your products, a buying guide that makes the comparison for them beats a longer description.

Signal 6: What does it actually cost to get this?

Two-minute test: look at a product page and see whether a shopper can work out the delivery cost and the delivery window without adding anything to the cart.

The fix: put both on the page. Extra costs are the most cited removable reason for abandonment in Baymard's abandonment research, named by 40% of shoppers who left, and 67% of benchmarked sites give no estimate of total order cost near the buy button. Shoppers rarely object to paying for shipping. They object to finding out at the last step.

Signal 7: What happens if it does not fit?

Two-minute test: from a product page, count the clicks to your return policy. If it is more than one, it is too many.

The fix: a single line near the price stating the return window and who pays return shipping, linked to the full policy. Baymard's research found 60% of shoppers look for return information on the product page itself, 44% of sites do not display or link it there, and 13% of abandoners walked away because the return policy did not satisfy them.

Signal 8: Did anyone actually write your policy pages?

Two-minute test: open your refund, shipping, privacy, and terms pages and read them. Shopify's generated templates are a starting draft, and they leave bracketed placeholders behind.

The fix: rewrite them in your own words with your real numbers in place. Shoppers who reach a policy page are close to buying and looking for a reason to feel safe. A page still saying the equivalent of "insert your address here" gives them the opposite.

Signals 9 to 12: where high traffic and no sales meet

Signal 9: Does the price look decided or imported?

Two-minute test: scan your product list for prices ending in stray cents. A shop full of items at $16.08, $9.53, and $23.41 reads as a catalogue nobody has touched since import.

The fix: round to prices a person would choose. It takes twenty minutes and it changes how considered the whole shop feels.

Signal 10: Can they ask a question and get an answer?

Two-minute test: find the fastest route from a product page to a real answer about shipping. If it is a contact form and a wait, that is your answer.

The fix: a visible FAQ first, then chat if you want it. Shoppers do not object to chat as such, as the data in the post on whether customers actually hate chatbots shows. They object to chat that cannot answer. A bot that does not know your shipping times, return window, and sizing is a signal 5 problem wearing a widget, which is why the chatbot work starts with writing the answers.

Signal 11: Does checkout look like it is handling money properly?

Two-minute test: buy something from your own store on your phone with a real card, then refund yourself. Count the taps, and check that guest checkout is on and the payment methods your customers use are enabled.

The fix: whatever that walk-through surfaced. In Baymard's abandonment data, 19% of shoppers left because they did not trust the site with their card details and 18% left because the site wanted them to create an account. On badges: a padlock graphic lifts how secure a page feels by a small amount, but it does not answer whether you will refund someone. Fix the information first, then add the badge if you like.

Signal 12: Do they know what happens after they click buy?

Two-minute test: read your order confirmation email. Does it say when the order ships, where from, and how tracking arrives?

The fix: one sentence on the product page and the same sentence in the confirmation. "Orders ship within two business days from Manila, and you will get a tracking link when they do." Certainty is what you are selling here.

Here is the whole audit in one place. Work down it with a notepad, and write the finding rather than fixing as you go.

Order of impact for a store under a thousand sessions a month: signal 2, then 7, then 6, then 4. Business identity, returns, shipping cost, reviews. All four are free, all four are writing rather than design, and together they answer the questions a cautious first-time shopper is actually holding.

Three things to skip. A theme redesign, because it changes everything at once and you will never learn what worked. Countdown timers and stock counters that are not true, because they read as exactly the kind of store you are trying not to look like. And changing six things in one weekend, because at your session volume you cannot attribute the result to any of them.

Wrapping up

Three things to take away from a month of Shopify traffic but no sales. Do the arithmetic first, because a store with 300 sessions and no orders is usually a store with 300 sessions, not a broken one. Rule out bots and mismatched traffic second, since those are ten-minute checks that can end the investigation early. Only then work the twelve signals, in visit order, one at a time.

Most of what makes a small store feel unsafe is missing information rather than bad design. A shopper who cannot find your return window, cannot see the delivery cost, cannot tell who runs the shop, and cannot find a single review has not judged your taste. They have run out of reasons to continue, and they leave without telling you why.

The honest part is that this work is slow, and none of it produces a chart that spikes on Monday. You will fix four things, wait a month for enough sessions to mean something, then fix four more. That is the actual job, and it is why so many services would rather sell you a redesign. Start with your footer and your return policy: the two cheapest signals on the list, and the two that most often turn out to be missing.

Frequently asked questions

If you're still unsure after reading these, just send the question.

How many sessions do I need before I can tell if my Shopify store has a conversion problem? +

Roughly 1,000 sessions before a zero means anything. At the 1.4% Shopify average, 200 sessions predicts about three orders and 500 predicts about seven, so a run of no sales at those volumes is normal variation rather than proof of a broken store. Below 1,000 sessions, spend your time on the free trust fixes instead of on testing changes you cannot measure.

Is 300 visitors and no sales normal for a new Shopify store? +

Yes. An average Shopify store would produce about four orders from 300 sessions, and plenty of perfectly healthy stores produce zero from a stretch that size. It only becomes a signal when the sessions are human, on target, and still not adding anything to the cart.

How do I know if my Shopify traffic is bots? +

Add the Human or bot session dimension to any sessions report in your Shopify admin, then filter it to Human. Bot filtering only applies to data from October 7, 2025 onward, so older sessions cannot be reclassified. New stores often see automated traffic concentrated in a few data centre cities, so a Sessions by location report topped by places you have never marketed to is a clue.

Do trust badges actually increase Shopify sales? +

A little, and less than most stores hope. Research on checkout security has consistently found that a padlock or a recognisable security logo raises how safe a page feels, which is not the same as answering the shopper's real question. A visible return policy, a shipping cost, and a contact address do more work than any badge, so fix those first and add the badge afterwards.

Should I redesign my Shopify store if I have traffic but no sales? +

Almost never as the first move. A redesign is expensive, slow, and changes so many things at once that you cannot tell what helped. Work through the trust signals first, since most of them are information you are missing rather than layout you got wrong.

How many reviews does a product need before shoppers will buy it? +

Fewer than most owners assume, and the first few matter most because they move the product from zero social proof to some. Recency matters as much as the count: a page whose newest review is eighteen months old reads worse than a page with three reviews from this quarter.

Will adding a chatbot fix traffic but no sales? +

Only one of the twelve signals, and only if the answers exist for it to give. A bot that cannot state your shipping times, return window, and sizing is a missing-information problem wearing a widget. Write the answers first, then put a bot in front of them.

How long should I wait after a change before judging whether it worked? +

Measure sample size, not calendar days. A week of 40 sessions tells you nothing, while 1,000 sessions across a month is enough to see a real move. Change one thing at a time so the result is attributable to something.
