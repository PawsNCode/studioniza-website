One Black Friday Page, Every Year: How to Build a BFCM Landing Page That Ranks Before November

Black Friday 2026 runs November 27 to November 30. This is how to build one Shopify landing page now, at a URL you keep forever, so it is indexed and earning positions before the searches start climbing in late October.

Black Friday 2026 lands on November 27. That is eleven weeks from today.

Most Shopify owners will build their Black Friday landing page in the third week of November, three or four days before the sale, once the discounts are finally decided. The page goes live. Google crawls it eventually. It ranks for nothing. In December it gets deleted, and the following year the same store does the same thing again.

That is a sequencing problem, not a discipline problem. A Black Friday landing page for Shopify has to exist long before the deal does, because ranking is the slow part and picking a discount is the fast part. Google's own guidance on Black Friday and Cyber Monday pages says to create the page well before the sale so Googlebot has time to find and index it, and to use one recurring URL rather than a new one for each occurrence of the event.

This post is the page-level version of that advice. One URL you create today and keep forever. What to put on it in September when you have no offer to announce. The title and meta pattern. What FAQ content is worth in 2026, and what its schema is no longer worth. Where to link it from, and when. What changes on the morning of November 27. And what to leave on the page in December instead of deleting it.

None of this needs an app or a page builder. It needs one page, made once, and eleven weeks of patience.

Why a Black Friday page built in November cannot rank in time

The demand is not in question. Shopify merchants generated $14.6 billion over the 2025 BFCM weekend, with 81 million-plus customers, an average cart of $114.70, and more than 94,900 merchants recording their highest-selling day ever on the platform. Some of those were first-year stores.

What is in question is whether search can deliver any of it to you. Searches for branded and category Black Friday terms start climbing in late October and peak in the last week of November. A page published on November 20 has to be discovered, crawled, indexed, and then evaluated against pages that have been sitting at the same address for three or four years. Google does not owe your new URL a shortcut because your sale starts Friday.

So the honest framing is this. The page is not the thing that wins the sale. The discount and the email list win the sale. The page is what lets organic search contribute anything at all, and it only contributes if it has been alive long enough to be trusted. Eleven weeks is enough. Eleven days is not.

One more honest limit before you build anything. You are not going to rank for "black friday." That term belongs to marketplaces and deal aggregators with a decade of links. What is genuinely winnable for a small store is your brand name plus black friday, and category-qualified phrases like black friday deals on the specific thing you sell. Aim there. Pick one URL and keep it forever

Google's recommendation for recurring events is specific and unambiguous: use a recurring URL, not a new URL for each occurrence, and give it a meaningful path that reflects the event and gets reused every year. The example in Google's guidance is to use /sale/black-friday rather than /sale/2020/black-friday. The ecommerce URL structure documentation says the same thing in general terms: use long-term, persistent URLs, with descriptive words in the path.

The reason is boring and powerful. Links, crawl history, and ranking signals attach to an address. A URL that survives four Black Fridays has four years of accumulation behind it. Four separate year-stamped URLs each have one season, and then they die.

Should it be a Shopify page or a collection?

Both, but they do different jobs, and most of the confusion in Shopify forums comes from trying to make one thing do both.

A page at /pages/black-friday holds copy, category links, an email form, and an FAQ. It is where the reading and the ranking happen. A collection at /collections/black-friday holds the actual discounted products. Collection descriptions accept HTML, but most themes render only a short block of it above the product grid, so long-form content there is usually wasted.

For a store under a few hundred products, build one page as the hub and one collection as the shelf, and have the page link to the collection. Do not build three. One thing worth checking before November: on some themes a new collection shows up inside the storefront filter menu, which looks strange in September when the sale is two months away. Preview your filters after you create it.

What to do with last year's /black-friday-2025 URL

If you already have a year-stamped page from a previous season, redirect it to the permanent one and consolidate the history. On Shopify this has a quirk that trips people up: redirects only work from broken URLs. If the old address still loads a real page, the redirect will not fire. You have to unpublish or delete the old page first, then create the redirect under Content, Menus, View URL Redirects.

The same rule creates a second trap. If you hide a collection and redirect it, then make that collection visible again next year, Shopify automatically deletes the redirect, because redirects only exist for URLs returning a 404. Put a note in your calendar. Our guide to Shopify 301 redirects covers the rest of the mechanics.

What goes on the page today, before you have a deal

This is the part nobody explains, and it is the reason most owners wait. They think the page needs the offer. It does not. It needs to be useful and specific about something, and in September there are six things you already know.

1. The dates. Write them plainly. Your Black Friday sale runs November 27 to November 30, 2026. You know this now. It is the single most searched piece of information about any store's sale, and almost nobody publishes it early.

2. An email capture. One field, one honest promise. Early access, one email, nothing else. This is the page's only conversion goal for the next eight weeks, and it is the reason the page pays for itself even if it ranks for nothing.

3. Last year's highlights, if you have them. What sold out, roughly what the discount range was, how fast the best sellers went. If this is your first BFCM, leave the block out. Do not invent a history.

4. Links to the three or four categories you will actually discount. This is what makes the page eligible for category-qualified searches, and it gives a shopper somewhere to go in September. If one of those categories has a buying guide, link that too.

5. A social proof block. Real reviews from the products you plan to discount. If your review count is thin, the eight weeks before BFCM are exactly when to fix that, which is what collecting reviews before Black Friday is about.

6. An FAQ with real questions. Shipping cutoffs, whether discount codes stack, whether the sale applies to already-reduced items, what the returns window is over the holidays. These are the questions your inbox gets every year.

What does not go on the page yet: a countdown timer to a sale you have not finalized, invented discount percentages, or a page that says only "deals coming soon" with nothing else on it. That last one is the most common version, and it is a thin page with a nice banner.

The title, meta, and FAQ pattern that does not fake an offer

The URL stays fixed. The title and meta description change three times a year, which is fine, because those fields are cheap to edit and carry no accumulated history.

Here is the pattern, with your brand and category dropped in. Before the sale: Black Friday 2026 at [Brand]: Sale Dates and Early Access. During: [Brand] Black Friday Sale: Up to 30% Off [Category]. After: [Brand] Black Friday: Dates and Early Access for Next Year. Shopify's page title field accepts up to 70 characters, but aim under 60 so it does not get cut off, and keep the meta description near 160.

Now the schema part, and this is where most Black Friday checklists you will read this autumn are out of date.

FAQ rich results are gone. Google's documentation changelog records that FAQ rich results stopped appearing in Google Search on May 7, 2026, and that the feature documentation was removed in June 2026. Every guide still telling you to add FAQ schema so your listing expands in the results is describing something that no longer happens. FAQPage markup remains valid schema.org and other systems still read it, so keeping it is reasonable. Add it because the visible answers help a shopper. Do not add it expecting a SERP feature, and make sure the markup matches the visible text word for word.

Do not put invented offer markup on the landing page. A hub page is not a product page, and a Product or Offer block with placeholder prices on it is a fabrication. Sale pricing markup belongs on the product pages, which is the next section.

How do you link to it, and what changes on November 27?

The one homepage link

Google's guidance asks you to link the landing page from your home page or somewhere similar so that both users and Google find it faster. Do that now, and do it quietly. A footer link or a small text link is enough in September. A full-width hero banner announcing Black Friday eleven weeks early makes your store look confused.

Save the main navigation for November. Nav placement is the loudest internal signal you have, and it reads as an announcement to shoppers. Add it in the first week of November, remove it in the first week of December. In between, link the page naturally from any seasonal blog posts you publish, and make sure your support chat knows the page exists so it can answer sale questions before the sale, which is one of the items on the chatbot holiday prep checklist.

Swap day

On November 27 you change the page's content, not its address. The teaser block becomes the live offer. The category links become links into the live sale collection. The title and meta description pick up the real discount. Then use the URL Inspection tool in Search Console to request a recrawl so the updated content is picked up quickly rather than whenever the next scheduled crawl arrives.

The sale pricing markup goes on your product pages. Google supports a start and end for a sale price using validFrom together with either validThrough or priceValidUntil, in ISO 8601 format with the time and timezone included. Google's merchant listing documentation also carries a warning worth writing down: a listing may not display if priceValidUntil indicates a date in the past. Set real dates, and clear them when the sale ends.

What to leave on the page after Cyber Monday

Do not delete it. Do not let it 404. Do not redirect it to the homepage. Those three moves are the reason most Shopify stores are permanently starting over, and the question "should I delete my Black Friday page now that the sale is done" is the most common thing owners ask in December.

What stays: the URL, one line saying the sale has ended, when it runs next year (late November 2027 is specific enough), the email capture, the FAQ, and the category links. That is a genuinely useful page for someone who searches your brand plus black friday in March, and it is the version of the page that quietly holds its position for eleven months.

What goes: the discount percentages, the countdown, the urgency copy, and the navigation link. On the product side, clear validThrough and priceValidUntil or reset them, because a stale past date can stop the listing from showing.

Expect the page to get close to zero traffic from January to September. That is not the page failing. Seasonal pages are dormant most of the year by definition, and the whole point of keeping one address is that the dormancy is free while the accumulation is not.

If you retired a year-stamped page earlier and hid the old collection to make the redirect work, put a reminder in next year's calendar. The moment you make that collection visible again, Shopify deletes the redirect on its own.

Wrapping up

Three things carry the whole approach. Pick one URL and never change it, because Google explicitly recommends recurring URLs for recurring events and because every address you abandon takes its history with it. Publish the page now with the six things you already know, rather than waiting for a discount you have not decided. And in December, change the content instead of deleting the page.

What that leaves you with is a Black Friday landing page for Shopify that gets a little stronger every year without any additional work, which is unusual in this business. Most seasonal effort resets. This one does not.

Be realistic about the first season. If you build this page today, it will not outrank a national retailer in November. It will get indexed, it will pick up branded and category-qualified impressions, and it will collect email addresses for eight weeks before the sale, which is probably worth more to a first-year store than the organic clicks. The compounding shows up in year two and year three.

The work left to you is the part I cannot do from here: deciding which three or four categories you will actually discount, writing FAQ answers that match your real shipping cutoffs, and putting the November swap on a calendar so it does not get lost in the week everything else is happening. An hour this weekend covers the build. Eleven weeks covers the rest.

Frequently asked questions

If you're still unsure after reading these, just send the question.

When should I create my Black Friday landing page for Shopify? +

As early as you can, and September is not too early. Google's own guidance for Black Friday and Cyber Monday pages is to create the page well before the sale so Googlebot has time to discover and index it. Publishing on September 6 leaves eleven weeks before BFCM 2026 begins on November 27, which is enough runway to get indexed, gather a few internal links, and start collecting impressions.

Should my Black Friday landing page URL include the year? +

No. Google recommends a recurring URL for events that repeat, using the example of /sale/black-friday rather than /sale/2020/black-friday. A year-stamped URL throws away its crawl history, its links, and its ranking every January. Put the year in the title tag and the on-page copy instead, where it costs nothing to change.

Can a small Shopify store rank for “black friday” on its own? +

Almost certainly not, and it is not a useful goal. The head term belongs to marketplaces, deal aggregators, and national retailers with years of link history. What a small store can win is branded and category-qualified search: your brand name plus black friday, and phrases like black friday deals on your specific product category.

Do I still need FAQ schema now that Google dropped FAQ rich results? +

FAQ rich results stopped appearing in Google Search on May 7, 2026, and Google removed the feature documentation in June 2026. FAQPage markup is still valid schema.org and other systems still parse it, so keeping it is reasonable. Just add it because the visible answers help a shopper, not because you expect an expandable box in the search results.

What happens if I delete my Black Friday page after Cyber Monday? +

You lose whatever rankings, links, and crawl history the page earned, and you start from zero next year. Every store that deletes and rebuilds is permanently eleven months behind the store that kept one URL. Change the content in December, keep the address.

How many Black Friday pages should a small Shopify store have? +

One hub page, plus one sale collection that the hub links to. Category-specific Black Friday pages are worth building only once a category has real depth and its own search demand, which for most first-year and second-year stores is not yet true. Three thin pages compete with each other and rank for nothing.
