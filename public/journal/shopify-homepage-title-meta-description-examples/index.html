Shopify Homepage Title and Meta Description: 12 Examples That Fit the Character Limits

Twelve homepage titles and meta descriptions written out in full, grouped by store type, with the character counts done. Plus the formula behind them and why Google sometimes ignores what you wrote.

You are in Online Store then Preferences with two empty boxes in front of you. One is labelled homepage title, the other is meta description, and neither one tells you what a good answer looks like.

That is the real problem with the Shopify homepage title and meta description. Every guide explains what a meta tag is. Almost none show you a finished one written for a store shaped like yours.

So this post is twelve of them. Six store types, two full rewrites each, with the character counts already done. Underneath the examples is the formula they all follow, the limits that genuinely apply, and the reason Google sometimes throws out what you wrote and shows your navigation menu instead.

Worth saying first: your homepage is the only page on the store that plays by different rules. On a product page you describe a product. On a collection page you describe a category. On the homepage you describe an entire business in about fifty characters, to someone who has never heard of you, in a list of nine results that all look similar.

That is genuinely harder, which is why so many new Shopify stores type the store name into the title field and move on.

Where the homepage title and meta description actually live in Shopify

Both fields sit in one place: Online Store > Preferences, under the section headed Social sharing image and SEO.

That section name trips people up. Older guides call it "Title and meta description," so owners go hunting in the theme customizer or in Settings > Store details instead. Store details holds your legal store name, a different field doing a different job.

Shopify's own limits on those two boxes are worth knowing before you write anything. The Shopify Help Center caps the page title at 70 characters, recommends keeping it to 60 or fewer, and recommends around 160 characters for the description.

Once you save, check your work in the page source rather than in Google. Open your homepage, right-click, choose View Page Source, and search for <title> and <meta name="description">. If those match what you typed, your store is set up correctly. Anything different in search results after that is Google's decision, not a Shopify problem.

You do not need an app for this. Filling two text boxes properly takes about twenty minutes, and any service charging a subscription for that specific job is charging you for a subscription.

What are the character limits for a Shopify homepage title and meta description?

There are two different sets of limits, and mixing them up is where most of the confusion comes from. Shopify limits what you can type. Google limits what people see.

Field Shopify's limit Write to What Google shows
Homepage title 70 characters, hard stop 50 to 60 characters Truncated to fit the device width, so noticeably shorter on a phone
Homepage meta description 160 characters recommended 130 to 155 characters Cut around 920 pixels on desktop and around 680 pixels on mobile

Google is explicit that there is no length limit on a meta description and that the snippet is simply truncated as needed to fit the device. The same is true of the title. Character counts are a proxy for pixel width, which is why two descriptions of the same length can be cut differently: a capital W takes far more room than a lowercase i.

Shopify's own guidance puts real numbers on that band. Text tends to get cut around 920 pixels on desktop, which usually lands between 150 and 160 characters, and around 680 pixels on mobile, which is closer to 110 to 120 characters.

The practical rule: write for the mobile cut, not the desktop one. Put the brand, the category, and the one detail that matters inside the first 110 characters of the description. Everything after that is a bonus that most of your audience will never read.

The same discipline applies to every other page on the store, and it is covered in more depth in how to write Shopify meta descriptions that earn the click.

The formula: brand, then category, then differentiator

Every good homepage title in this post is built from three slots.

Brand. Your store name, in its shortest usable form. Not the legal entity, not "Ltd," not a tagline.

Category. What you sell, in the words a stranger would use to search for it. "Linen clothing," not "curated essentials." This is the slot most stores skip entirely, and skipping it is the single most common reason a homepage never ranks for anything except its own name.

Differentiator. One thing that is true, specific, and checkable. Made in Vermont. No minimum order. Sizes 24 to 40. Ships in 48 hours. Not "quality products" and not "since 2019," unless being established is genuinely the reason people buy.

Google's guidance supports treating the homepage differently from every other page. Its documentation on influencing title links warns against vague descriptors like "Home" and says the title element on a site's home page is a reasonable place to include extra information about the site. That is permission, from Google, to use the differentiator slot here in a way you would not on a product page.

Pick one separator, a pipe or a dash or a comma, and use it consistently. Then delete anything not doing work: "Official Site," "Shop Online," and "Welcome to" all cost characters and earn nothing.

The meta description runs the same three beats with more room, plus one concrete term of doing business: a shipping window, a return policy, a price floor. Google's snippet documentation is direct about the split, recommending a site-level description on the home page and page-level descriptions everywhere else. If you have already applied the Shopify product title formula to your catalogue, this is the same thinking scaled up from one SKU to an entire business.

12 Shopify homepage title and meta description examples by store type

The store names below are invented. The patterns are not. Each pair shows a different slot carrying the weight, and every title and description is inside the limits from the table above, with the counts shown so you can see where the ceiling sits.

Single-product store

One product means one job: name the thing and say why yours is worth the extra step. The differentiator slot does almost all the work here, because the category slot is over in two words.

Title (50 characters): Wildroot Beard Oil | Cold-Pressed, Made in Vermont
Meta description (140 characters): One beard oil, made properly. Cold-pressed jojoba and cedar, blended in small batches in Vermont. Free US shipping over $35, 90-day returns.

Title (51 characters): Pocketleaf | The Refillable Notebook That Lies Flat
Meta description (131 characters): A refillable A5 notebook with a lay-flat spine and 120gsm paper. Refills ship for $6. Made in Portugal, sent worldwide from Lisbon.

Boutique or apparel

Apparel homepages compete against thousands of near-identical results, so the differentiator has to be something a shopper can check: a size range, a fabric, a country of manufacture.

Title (47 characters): Marlow & Fen | Linen Clothing for Warm Climates
Meta description (132 characters): Breathable linen dresses, shirts, and trousers cut for hot weather. Sizes 6 to 24, made in Portugal. Free UK returns within 30 days.

Title (45 characters): Hollis Supply | Vintage Denim, Sizes 24 to 40
Meta description (136 characters): Hand-picked vintage denim from the 80s and 90s, measured and photographed on real bodies. Sizes 24 to 40. One of each, so it moves fast.

Subscription

Subscription shoppers are asking one silent question before they click: how hard is it to stop? Answering that in the description removes the objection before the page loads.

Title (49 characters): Ember Roast | Coffee Subscription, Roasted Weekly
Meta description (143 characters): Single-origin coffee roasted Thursday and shipped Friday. Whole bean or ground, pause any week, cancel any time. From $17 a bag, free shipping.

Title (46 characters): The Stem Club | Flower Delivery Across Chicago
Meta description (139 characters): Seasonal stems from Midwest growers, delivered to Chicago homes every other Tuesday. Skip a week whenever you like. Plans from $42 a month.

B2B or wholesale

A buyer is scanning for terms, not for adjectives. Minimum order, payment terms, and lead time are the differentiators, and putting them in the snippet filters out the wrong traffic before it costs you anything.

Title (49 characters): Northbay Packaging | Wholesale Boxes, No Minimums
Meta description (137 characters): Wholesale kraft mailers and shipping boxes for small brands. No minimum order, net-30 for approved accounts, ships from Ohio in two days.

Title (46 characters): Ashgrove Textiles | Organic Cotton by the Roll
Meta description (142 characters): GOTS-certified organic cotton jersey and fleece, sold by the roll to clothing labels. Swatch packs are $12. Trade pricing on account approval.

Local pickup or service area

If you serve one city, say the city. It costs about fifteen characters and it is the strongest differentiator a small store gets, because it disqualifies every national competitor in the same result.

Title (49 characters): Fernwood Plants | Houseplants in Portland, Oregon
Meta description (137 characters): Houseplants, pots, and soil from a small shop on SE Division. Order online for same-day pickup, or local delivery across Portland for $8.

Title (47 characters): Rye & Ember | Sourdough Preorder in East Austin
Meta description (139 characters): Naturally leavened sourdough, baked Wednesday to Sunday in East Austin. Preorder by 6pm the night before and collect at the shop or market.

General or dropship catalogue

This is the hardest homepage to write, because the products are not exclusive to you. When the catalogue cannot be the differentiator, the terms of doing business have to be: shipping origin, return window, warranty, how the picking is done.

Title (50 characters): Trailhead Gear Co | Camping Kit, Ships in 48 Hours
Meta description (140 characters): Tested camping and hiking gear chosen by people who use it. Ships from a US warehouse in 48 hours. Free returns for 60 days, no restock fee.

Title (49 characters): Lumen Home | Small-Space Lighting, 30-Day Returns
Meta description (140 characters): Lamps and fixtures picked for apartments and rentals. Every listing shows real room dimensions. Free 30-day returns and a two-year warranty.

Read them in a batch and the shared structure gets obvious. Not one of them opens with "Welcome to." Not one of them uses the word "quality." Every single description contains at least one number.

Why does Google rewrite a homepage title or description you wrote yourself?

Because your title tag is one input among several, not an instruction. Google builds the title link from the title element, the main visible heading, the og:title tag, other large and prominent text, anchor text pointing at the page, and WebSite structured data. Snippets are drawn primarily from page content, and your description gets used only when Google decides it describes the page better than the content does.

When a rewrite happens, it is usually one of three things.

1. The homepage has almost no text. This is the common one on Shopify. A hero image, a slider, a product grid, a row of trust icons, a footer. When Google needs a sentence and there is not one, it takes whatever text exists. That is how stores end up with a snippet made of navigation labels, a login prompt, cookie banner copy, or a list of collection links.

2. The title does not match what is visibly on the page. Google checks the title element against the page's main heading and adjusts when they disagree.

3. There is no clear main heading. Google names this one: when several headings share the same size and weight, it may just take the first.

The fixes, in the order worth trying them:

Add two or three real sentences of text to the homepage, near the top. This is the fix that actually works and the one most stores skip, and it belongs to the wider question of what to put above the fold on your Shopify homepage.

If one specific block keeps getting pulled into your snippet, mark that block with the data-nosnippet attribute rather than hiding it with CSS. Google supports the attribute for exactly this, and hiding an element with CSS only changes what visitors see, not what Google reads.

Then make sure exactly one heading on the homepage is unmistakably the largest thing on the page.

Worth being honest about the ceiling here: none of this is a control. Google states plainly that it cannot manually change snippets for individual sites. You are improving the odds, not setting a value.

The store-name-as-title trap

The most common homepage title on a new Shopify store is the store name and nothing else. It feels correct, because the brand is the thing you care about most. It is also the one piece of information already on the screen.

Google generates a site name for your store automatically, from content on your homepage, and displays it above the title link as a separate element. So a result for a store titled only "Marlow & Fen" reads "Marlow & Fen" and then, underneath it, "Marlow & Fen." One of those was free. The other one cost you the whole title.

Google also documents that it may drop a site name from the title link when it duplicates the one already being shown. That explains a pattern that turns up constantly in Shopify support threads: an owner writes a proper homepage title, confirms it in the page source, and Google keeps showing the bare shop name. Nothing is broken. The title is losing to a site name Google already holds.

Three things to do about it:

Never write a homepage title that is only your store name. Give Google something to show that it does not already have.

Add WebSite structured data to your homepage with a name property, and an alternateName if people shorten your store name in conversation. Google says that block is the strongest signal for which site name it picks.

Keep the store name in the title, but put it at the end, where it works as a signature rather than as the message.

Wrapping up

Three things to take away. First, the homepage title and meta description are two boxes in Online Store > Preferences, capped at 70 and roughly 160 characters, and they take twenty minutes and no app. Second, the format that works is brand, category, differentiator, with the description carrying one concrete term of doing business. Third, if Google keeps ignoring what you wrote, the cause is almost always a homepage with too little text on it rather than a mistake in the fields.

What this will not do is move you up the results page on its own. A better title and description change how many people click an impression you already have, not how many impressions you get. On a homepage with a few hundred monthly impressions that difference is small in absolute terms, and still worth having, because it compounds for as long as the page ranks.

The harder part is the differentiator. Writing it forces you to answer why someone should buy from you rather than from the store above you, in about fifteen characters, without exaggerating. Most owners find the sentence is not hard to write once they know it, and that knowing it is the actual work.

If you have ten minutes right now, open your homepage in a search result and compare what Google is displaying against what sits in Preferences. That gap is your list. Frequently asked questions

If you're still unsure after reading these, just send the question.

What is the character limit for a Shopify homepage title and meta description? +

Shopify caps the page title field at 70 characters and recommends staying at 60 or fewer so search engines do not shorten it. For the meta description, Shopify recommends around 160 characters. Google itself has no hard character limit on either field and truncates by pixel width instead, so treat character counts as a working proxy rather than a rule.

Is the Shopify homepage meta description a ranking factor? +

No. The meta description does not directly affect where your homepage ranks. It affects how many people click once you are already ranking, which is a separate and often faster win. Google also treats it as one candidate for the snippet rather than a guaranteed one.

Should my store name go at the start or the end of the homepage title? +

Usually the end, so the first words describe what you sell. The exception is a store name people already search for by name, where leading with the brand makes the result easier to recognise. Either way, the store name should never be the whole title.

How long does it take for a new Shopify homepage title to appear in Google? +

Anywhere from a few days to a few weeks, because Google has to recrawl and reprocess the page. You can speed it up by requesting indexing through the URL Inspection tool in Google Search Console. If nothing has changed after several weeks, check the page source before assuming it is a crawl delay.

Can I use the same meta description on my homepage and my About page? +

No, give each page its own. Google says identical descriptions across a site are not helpful when individual pages appear in results. The homepage description should describe the whole business, while every other page describes only itself.

Do I need an SEO app to set my Shopify homepage title and meta description? +

No. Both fields are built into Shopify at Online Store then Preferences, and filling them takes about twenty minutes. Apps become useful for editing hundreds of product pages in bulk, not for two text boxes on the homepage.
